# Father Involvement in Family Dynamics: A Qualitative Exploration of Perceptions and Cultural Influences

**Authors:** Bereket Merkine Gebresilase; Meiping Wang; Zhang Chuanxia; Esayas Teshome Taddese; Zebdewos Zekarias Elka; Yohannes Bisa Biramo

**Affiliations:** College of General Education, Shandong Xiehe University, Jinan, China; School of Psychology, Shandong Normal University, Jinan, China; Faculty of Education and Liberal Arts, INTI International University, Nilai, Malaysia; Department of Psychology, Wolaita Sodo University, Sodo, Ethiopia

**PMC:** PMC12620457

---

## Abstract

This qualitative phenomenological study examined how Ethiopian fathers from the Wolaita Zone perceive their paternal roles and the cultural factors that shape those perceptions. Thirty semi-structured interviews were conducted with fathers and analyzed using thematic analysis (NVivo 12 software). Data were transcribed, back-translated from Wolaitato to English.

The study revealed three primary themes:
1. Fathers defining involvement primarily through financial support, social participation, and supervisory roles over maternal caregiving
2. Fathers largely distancing themselves from household tasks (cooking, childcare) viewed as culturally incompatible with masculinity
3. Cultural tradition and religious beliefs cited as the basis for gendered role divisions

A minority of urban, educated fathers reported broader involvement including play, quality time, and shared household responsibilities — suggesting gradual cultural shifts in urban contexts.

The findings call for culturally grounded, context-specific interventions including community-based programs, school-based father engagement initiatives, and policy reforms such as expanded paternity leave.

---

## Introduction

### Background

The role of fathers in family dynamics has received increasing attention in developmental psychology and family studies. Contemporary scholarship recognizes that paternal involvement is critical to children's cognitive, emotional, and social development. Frameworks used: attachment theory, ecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner), and self-determination theory.

In sub-Saharan African contexts, including Ethiopia, paternal roles are strongly shaped by cultural norms, religious teachings, and patriarchal social structures. Ethiopian society — particularly in rural areas — traditionally assigns men the role of breadwinner and external household representative, while women manage domestic duties and direct childcare.

The Wolaita Zone of southern Ethiopia exhibits a mix of traditional agrarian culture and emerging urbanization — an ideal case study for examining paternal role perceptions.

---

## Methods

### Study Design

Qualitative phenomenological design to capture lived experiences and perceptions of Ethiopian fathers.

### Participants

Thirty fathers from the Wolaita Zone recruited through purposive sampling. Both rural/urban residents and both formally/less formally educated individuals.

### Data Collection

Semi-structured interviews conducted in Wolaitato. Topics: perceived paternal responsibilities, participation in domestic tasks, views on childcare, cultural and religious influences, attitudes toward change.

### Data Analysis

Audio-recorded interviews, transcribed, back-translated from Wolaitato to English. Thematic analysis with NVivo 12 software. Rigor ensured through member checking, peer debriefing, and reflexivity.

---

## Results

### Theme 1: Paternal Role Perceived Primarily as Financial Support and Social Representation

Majority of participants (approximately 20/30) defined fathering through financial provision, social participation, and supervisory oversight of maternal caregiving.

Representative quote:
> "Father engagement includes helping moms financially, addressing societal issues, and paying for children's education."

Fathers described primary duties as ensuring economic stability, acting as external community representatives, and supervising — rather than directly participating in — childrearing.

### Theme 2: Limited and Stigmatized Domestic Task Participation

Fathers largely avoided cooking, cleaning, and direct childcare, viewing these as culturally incompatible with masculinity. Social stigma was a significant barrier: participation in domestic tasks risked perceptions of weakness.

Representative quote:
> "Domestic tasks are not widely recognized by society as falling within men's responsibilities."

A minority of urban, educated fathers in dual-income households reported moderate involvement in homework supervision and occasional household assistance.

### Theme 3: Cultural Tradition and Religious Beliefs as Justification for Gendered Roles

Two main sources:
1. Inherited cultural tradition passed down through generations
2. Religious convictions framing gender roles as divinely ordained

Representative quotes:
> "The division of roles in the family is not just cultural, it is God's will."

> "This is how our fathers did it, and their fathers before them."

Rural participants more frequently invoked cultural tradition; some drew on religious justification. A small number of urban participants questioned these justifications.

---

## Discussion

### Key Insights

Findings confirm that paternal role perceptions in the Wolaita Zone are strongly shaped by patriarchal cultural norms and religious frameworks assigning breadwinning to men and domestic/caregiving responsibilities to women — consistent with broader literature on fatherhood in sub-Saharan Africa.

The ecological systems framework helps contextualize these findings: microsystem (family), mesosystem (community), exosystem (workplace, policy), and macrosystem (culture, religion) all reinforce traditional gender role divisions.

**Attachment theory implication:** Children benefit from secure attachments to fathers as well as mothers, and father absence/disengagement is associated with poorer outcomes in cognitive development, emotional regulation, and social competence.

**Urban gradient:** The minority of urban, educated fathers who reported broader involvement suggests that urbanization, increased female labor force participation, and formal education are gradually reshaping paternal role perceptions — consistent with modernization theories of gender role change.

---

## Conclusions

Ethiopian fathers in the Wolaita Zone predominantly perceive their role through the lens of financial provision, social representation, and supervisory oversight, with caregiving and domestic tasks viewed as women's responsibilities.

### Recommended Interventions

1. **Community-based programs** involving religious leaders and community elders to reframe paternal involvement as consistent with cultural and religious values
2. **School-based father engagement initiatives** creating peer networks normalizing broader paternal participation
3. **Healthcare-integrated father inclusion** in maternal and child health programs
4. **Policy reforms**: expanded paternity leave to signal institutional support for shared parenting

Promoting equitable parenting in this context requires working within — rather than against — local cultural frameworks, leveraging existing values of family responsibility and communal well-being to gradually expand the definition of fatherhood.
