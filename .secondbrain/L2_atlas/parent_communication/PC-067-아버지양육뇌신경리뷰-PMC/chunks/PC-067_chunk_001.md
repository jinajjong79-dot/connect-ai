# The Role of Paternal Involvement on Behavioral Sensitive Responses and Neurobiological Activations in Fathers: A Systematic Review

**Authors:** Michele Giannotti, Micol Gemignani, Paola Rigo, Paola Venuti, Simona De Falco

**Affiliations:** Department of Psychology and Cognitive Sciences, University of Trento; Department of Developmental Psychology and Socialisation, University of Padua

**Journal:** Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience, 2022 Mar 9;16:820884

**PMC:** PMC8959913

---

This systematic review examines the relationship between paternal involvement in childcare and both neurobiological responses and sensitive behaviors in fathers. The analysis encompassed peer-reviewed quantitative studies investigating paternal responses to infant stimuli at neurobiological or behavioral levels, with quantitative measurements of paternal involvement. Of 2,529 initial articles, 27 studies met inclusion criteria. Quality ratings indicated 10 studies used "fairly good" measures of paternal involvement, while 17 employed "good-standard" measures. Only 8 studies reported relevant findings linking involvement to neurobiological responses or sensitive behaviors. Key finding: "paternal involvement seems to play a significant role in modulating both the hormonal and the neural pathways associated with paternal behaviors" when high-standard measures are employed. The review suggests caregiving experience—rather than biological factors alone—may underpin adaptive paternal nurturance.

---

Contemporary sociocultural changes have increased paternal involvement in child-rearing. Research increasingly focuses on paternal roles and their influence on infant development. However, the association among neurobiological activations to infant stimuli, paternal behavioral sensitivity, and paternal involvement in childcare remains understudied.

Parental sensitivity, per Attachment Theory, involves recognizing and responding adequately to children's cues. Research traditionally emphasized maternal sensitivity but has expanded to examine paternal contributions. Studies document that "sensitive fathers respond accurately to children's signals and needs, showing appropriate attunement" across contexts. Paternal sensitivity correlates with positive child outcomes including cognitive functioning, emotion regulation, and attachment security.

Notably, fathers often display lower sensitivity levels than mothers, though this may reflect assessment methods originally developed for mothers. Father-child interactions feature distinctive characteristics: fathers emphasize stimulation and exploratory play more than mothers, including physical and rough-and-tumble activities. Yet paternal involvement in childcare represents a key factor potentially contributing to sensitive behaviors—a relationship poorly investigated to date.

The Parental Brain Model indicates key brain circuits activate when parents encounter infant stimuli. Fathers demonstrate functional brain adaptations alongside hormonal changes supporting caregiving. Research shows decreased testosterone levels in fathers, associated with enhanced nurturant behaviors. Elevated oxytocin, vasopressin, and prolactin correlate with greater paternal synchrony and responsiveness.

Neural imaging studies reveal fathers recruit systems involved in sensory processing, motivation, and empathy responding to infant cues. Brain regions showing activation include bilateral Inferior Frontal Gyrus, Anterior Insula, Temporo-Parietal Junction, and reward-related areas. Fathers demonstrate greater activation in reward and attachment-related regions responding to their own infants.

Fathers' neural responses rely more on socio-cognitive networks compared to mothers' limbic-focused activations. Yet variability related to parental involvement remains underexamined in neurobiological research.

Paternal involvement encompasses the quantity of time fathers engage with their child, availability, and caregiving responsibilities assumed. Research demonstrates links between paternal engagement and children's developmental outcomes. Greater paternal involvement associates with improved cognitive functioning, academic achievement, language development, and reduced externalizing behaviors.

Benefits extend to family contexts: paternal engagement positively impacts maternal health and child cognitive development. Paternal involvement buffers against maternal depression's adverse effects on child outcomes.

However, measurement heterogeneity across studies limits comparability. Different conceptualizations yield inconsistent operational definitions, with some studies measuring partial components or conflating distinct aspects into limited measures.

This review systematically examines:
1. How paternal involvement has been measured and its quality
2. Whether paternal involvement associates with neurobiological activation or sensitive behavioral responses in fathers

---

Systematic literature search across PsycInfo and PubMed databases through March 2021, following PRISMA guidelines. Search terms: "(Paternal OR Father) AND (Sensitivity OR Responsiveness) AND (Brain OR Hormones OR Neural OR Father-child interaction OR Child care)."

**Inclusion criteria:**
- Quantitative data reported
- Peer-reviewed journal publication
- Paternal sensitivity behaviors or neurobiological responses to infant stimuli assessed
- Fathers included in sample
- Human subjects
- Quantitative measurement of paternal involvement in childcare

**Exclusion criteria:**
- Parents with psychiatric diagnoses or children with atypical development
- Only adolescent samples
- Exclusively self-reported parental sensitivity/responsiveness

Initial screening: 2,529 records, 12 duplicates removed. Title/abstract screening excluded 2,385 articles. Of 132 full-text assessments, 27 met inclusion criteria.

Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool 2018 (MMAT) evaluated methodological quality. Overall agreement reached 93.75%.

---

27 studies met inclusion criteria: 11 (40.7%) addressed neurobiological aspects; 16 (59.3%) examined sensitive/responsive behaviors. Neurobiological studies measured hormonal responses (n=5; 18.5%) or neural activations via fMRI/EEG/fNIRS (n=6; 22.2%).

**"Fairly good" measures (10 studies):** Single or few items; poor methodological clarity; instruments not specifically developed for paternal involvement assessment.

**"Good" measures (17 studies):** Well-established validated questionnaires; structured interviews or daily journals; ad-hoc detailed question sets.

Commonly used instruments:
- Childcare Activity Scale
- Parental Responsibility Scale
- Parental Engagement Questionnaire
- Interaction/Accessibility Time Diary interview protocol

Significant findings:
- **Grossmann et al. (2002):** First-year caregiving composite index predicted father sensitive play at 24 months
- **Brown et al. (2012):** Paternal involvement associated with sensitivity at 3 years. Four involvement characteristics predicted sensitivity: sharing household/childcare responsibilities, weekend time with child, and breadth of caregiving activities.
- **Feldman (2000):** Significant associations between paternal involvement and sensitivity

Non-significant findings: Several studies found no association (Kazura 2000; Laflamme et al. 2002; Malmberg et al. 2016).
