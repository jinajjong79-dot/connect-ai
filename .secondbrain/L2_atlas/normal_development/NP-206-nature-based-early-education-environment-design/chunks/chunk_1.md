ABSTRACT

This research examines designing effective outdoor play and learning environments (OPLEs) for infants and toddlers (below 3 years). It bridges a significant gap in early childhood environmental design, as most outdoor play research focuses on children 3+. Using a three-phase methodology at Texas Tech University Early Head Start, the study identified 5 design domains and 20 specific outdoor learning zones that support holistic development in the youngest children.

Pre/post design intervention comparisons showed significant improvements across all five design domains.

INTRODUCTION

The problem: Outdoor play environments for children under 3 are typically underdesigned, unsafe, or poorly matched to developmental needs. Most playground design standards target 5-12 year olds. Early Head Start and infant-toddler centers often have either no outdoor space or minimal, equipment-based spaces that fail to support naturalistic learning.

Why under-3 outdoor design matters:
- Birth to age 3 represents the most rapid period of neural development
- Sensory-rich experiences drive synaptic formation
- Language, cognitive, motor, and social-emotional development are intertwined
- Natural materials (sand, water, mud, plants) offer superior sensory complexity vs. plastic equipment

Gap in literature: "Virtually all outdoor learning environment research focuses on preschoolers (3-5 years) or older. The 0-3 age group has been systematically neglected in design research."

METHODS

Phase 1: Narrative literature review
- Identified design principles from developmental research on 0-3 year olds
- Synthesized with general outdoor learning environment literature

Phase 2: Development of guidelines and assessment tool
- Identified 20 specific outdoor learning zones
- Created assessment instrument: Outdoor Play and Learning Environment Rating for Infants and Toddlers (OPLERIT)

Phase 3: Pre/post environmental assessment
- Site: Texas Tech University Early Head Start facility
- Assessment conducted before and after intentional redesign intervention
- Statistical comparison of zone quality ratings

FIVE DESIGN DOMAINS

Domain 1: Sensory Opportunities
Core sensory materials and activities:
- Sand and digging areas (tactile, proprioceptive)
- Water play areas (visual, tactile, auditory)
- Pathways with different textures (grass, bark, smooth stone, rough stone)
- Gardens with edible plants (taste, smell, touch)
- Wind and sound: windmills, bells, rustling plants
- "Sensory richness is the primary driver of neural development in the first 3 years"

Domain 2: Construction and Manipulation
Loose parts and building materials:
- Natural loose parts: sticks, stones, pinecones, shells
- Sand and mud for digging, filling, dumping
- Simple construction: stacking, arranging, transferring
- Age-appropriate tools: shovels, cups, small buckets
- "Loose parts play supports early STEM cognition: physics, mathematics, cause-effect"

Domain 3: Art, Language, and Literature
Creative and language-rich spaces:
- Wildlife habitat (bird feeders, butterfly gardens) — vocabulary development through naming
- Outdoor art (chalk surfaces, mud painting, leaf printing)
- Reading nooks: weather-protected, comfortable outdoor seating with books
- Pretend play structures (simple playhouses, sensory kitchens)
- "Language acquisition accelerates when connected to real-world sensory experience"

Domain 4: Physical Activity and Health
Movement and gross motor:
- Pathways for walking, running, crawling (varied surfaces and gradients)
- Gentle slopes and topography (balance, proprioception)
- Climbing elements scaled for under-3 (low heights, wide surfaces)
- Lawn areas for open running, rolling, tumbling
- Gardens for gross motor digging and carrying

Domain 5: Rest and Relaxation
Recovery and calming spaces:
- Shaded rest areas with comfortable seating (mats, hammocks)
- Quiet garden spaces away from active play
- Natural healing spaces: sensory gardens with lavender, chamomile, soft textures
- Caregiver seating for responsive observation
- "Outdoor rest spaces allow cortisol recovery and emotional regulation between active periods"

TWENTY OUTDOOR LEARNING ZONES

The 20 zones map onto the 5 domains and 4 developmental areas (social-emotional, language, cognitive, perceptual-motor):

1. Sand area (deep, 12-18 inches)
2. Water play area (sensory table or shallow water feature)
3. Mud kitchen
4. Digging patch
5. Garden beds (edible plants at child height)
6. Textured pathways
7. Open lawn
8. Gentle slope
9. Low climbing structure (max 18 inches for under-3)
10. Loose parts storage
11. Natural loose parts collection area
12. Outdoor art space
13. Bird and wildlife habitat
14. Sound garden
15. Wind elements
16. Outdoor reading nook
17. Pretend play area
18. Gross motor equipment (age-scaled)
19. Shaded rest space
20. Caregiver observation seating

RESULTS

Pre/post comparison at Texas Tech Early Head Start:
- All 5 design domains showed significant improvement after redesign
- Largest gains: Sensory Opportunities and Rest/Relaxation (domains most neglected initially)
- Staff reported increased ease of outdoor supervision
- Parent engagement with outdoor space increased

Key observations during assessment:
- Children showed sustained engagement in sensory activities (avg 8-12 minutes vs. 2-3 min with plastic equipment)
- Language output increased when children engaged with natural materials
- Emotional regulation difficulties decreased in outdoor sessions post-redesign

DISCUSSION

The 0-3 design gap has real consequences: under-designed outdoor spaces mean children miss the developmental benefits of outdoor nature play during the highest-plasticity period of their lives. This study provides an actionable framework for childcare centers to redesign existing spaces.

Key principles:
- Natural materials > manufactured equipment for under-3 development
- Sensory complexity drives engagement and learning
- Rest and recovery spaces are as important as active spaces
- Loose parts provide open-ended challenge appropriate across the 0-3 developmental range

CONCLUSION

Intentional nature-based design substantially enhances developmental opportunities for infants and toddlers in care settings. The 5-domain, 20-zone framework provides actionable guidelines for childcare facilities. Policy should require outdoor nature play environments meeting these standards as a condition of licensing for infant-toddler care.

Design Priority for Korean Early Childhood Settings:
- Most Korean daycare/어린이집 outdoor spaces are dominated by plastic playground equipment
- Nature-based loose parts spaces are rare but feasible within existing spaces
- Sand, water, garden, and texture pathway elements are low-cost high-impact additions
