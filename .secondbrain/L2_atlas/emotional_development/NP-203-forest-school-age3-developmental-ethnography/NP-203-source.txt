Title: Forest Schools as a Promising Method to Improve Nature Connectedness: An Ethnographic Evaluation of a Pilot Program for 3-Year-Old Children in Bradford, UK
PMC ID: PMC11447439
Year: 2024
Tier: 2 | Grade: A
Category: emotional_development
Keywords: forest school, age 3, ethnography, nature connectedness, social-emotional development, urban children, language development, EYFS

---

ABSTRACT

This ethnographic study evaluated a Forest School pilot program for 3-year-old children in Bradford, UK, examining developmental outcomes using the Early Years Foundation Stage (EYFS) framework. The study involved 20 children (ages 3) in 11 weekly sessions across two urban nursery settings serving ethnically diverse, economically deprived communities.

Key Findings:
- Significant improvements in confidence, emotional regulation, and social skills
- Enhanced verbal expression, especially for English language learners
- Increased motor skills in diverse natural environments
- Benefits extended to parents and nursery staff
- Forest Schools are "a promising and feasible method to improve nature connectedness" for urban children

INTRODUCTION

Forest School, originating in Scandinavia and established in the UK in the 1990s, involves regular child-initiated outdoor learning in woodland/nature settings. While evidence exists for older children, research on Forest School outcomes for very young children (age 3) is limited. This study addresses that gap in urban, deprived contexts where children have minimal woodland exposure.

EYFS framework domains assessed:
1. Personal, Social and Emotional Development (PSED)
2. Communication and Language
3. Physical Development
4. Mathematics
5. Literacy and Understanding the World
6. Expressive Arts and Design

METHODS

Design: Qualitative ethnographic study
Duration: 11 weekly sessions
Observation hours: 65 hours of participant observation
Interviews: 14 in-depth interviews with parents and staff
Sample: 20 children (age 3), their parents, nursery staff, Forest School leaders
Settings: Two urban nursery settings in Bradford
- Ethnically diverse, economically disadvantaged communities
- Staff-to-child ratio: 4:10
Location: Local parks and woodland areas

RESULTS

Personal, Social and Emotional Development (Primary domain — strongest findings):

Confidence and self-efficacy:
- Multiple children demonstrated increased risk-taking and independence
- Case example: child with extreme animal phobia overcame fear of ducks through gradual exposure
- Case example: previously shy child demonstrated leadership in peer groups
- "Children who were reluctant initially became enthusiastic advocates for outdoor activities by session 5"

Emotional regulation:
- Staff observed "reduced meltdowns and emotional outbursts" on Forest School days
- Parents reported children were "calmer but more talkative" after sessions
- Hypothesis: physical activity + natural sensory engagement reduces cortisol

Social skills:
- Cooperative play increased markedly in unstructured woodland settings
- Children negotiated rules and roles without adult facilitation
- N=20, but all children showed at least 1 documented social skills improvement

Communication and Language:
- Vocabulary acquisition through nature naming and description
- Particular benefit for English as Additional Language (EAL) children
- "Children who were reluctant to speak in nursery became verbal in the forest"
- EAL children used home language to express wonder, then transitioned to English naming

Physical Development:
- Balance, coordination, gross motor skills improved in varied terrain
- Risk assessment behaviors (looking before climbing, testing branch strength) observed
- Fine motor: manipulation of natural materials (sticks, mud, leaves, pinecones)

Mathematics:
- Counting, sorting, spatial concepts through natural materials
- Children spontaneously engaged in size comparison (big/small sticks, heavy/light stones)

Staff and Parent Secondary Benefits:
- 12 of 14 interviewed parents reported reduced anxiety about outdoor play
- 8 of 14 reported increased personal nature engagement
- Staff reported increased job satisfaction and professional identity

DISCUSSION

Mechanisms of benefit:
1. Autonomy: Child-initiated play in natural settings supports executive function development
2. Sensory richness: Multi-sensory natural environment activates wider neural networks than classroom
3. Risk and challenge: Appropriate challenge builds confidence and self-efficacy (matches risky play literature)
4. Adult facilitation style: Forest School practitioners use observation-first approach, minimal intervention

Relevance for urban children from disadvantaged backgrounds:
- Children with least nature access benefit most from Forest School
- Language development gains especially important for EAL children
- Social capital built between families (parents observed each other, formed connections)

Limitations:
- Small sample (N=20); cannot establish causation
- Short intervention period (10-11 sessions)
- Cannot distinguish Forest School benefits from general outdoor exposure effects
- Qualitative design: researcher may influence settings
- No control group

CONCLUSION

Forest Schools represent a "promising and feasible method to improve nature connectedness" for very young urban children with limited woodland experience. Benefits span all EYFS domains, with PSED (confidence, emotional regulation, social skills) showing the strongest evidence. Cities should integrate weekly Forest School sessions as standard in early years provision, particularly in deprived communities.

Practice Recommendations:
- Minimum 10 sessions needed to establish trust and rhythm
- 4:10 staff ratio essential for safety in woodland settings
- Qualified Forest School practitioners (Level 3+) should lead sessions
- Regular forest sessions (weekly) superior to occasional trips
