Title: Emotion-Specific Vocabulary and Its Relations to Emotion Knowledge and Behavioral Emotion Regulation in Preschoolers
PMC ID: PMC12887049
Year: 2026
Tier: 2 | Grade: A ★★
Category: emotional_development
Keywords: emotion vocabulary, emotion knowledge, emotion regulation, preschool, semantic depth, vocabulary size, behavioral regulation, German preschoolers

---

ABSTRACT

This cross-sectional study examined 197 German preschoolers (ages 4-6, mean 5.6 years) to investigate how emotion-specific vocabulary (size and depth) relates to emotion knowledge and behavioral emotion regulation. Key finding: Emotion-specific vocabulary explains unique variance in emotion knowledge beyond general vocabulary. Depth (precision of use) compensates for smaller vocabulary size. Vocabulary × depth interaction predicted positive emotion regulation.

Core message: "감정 단어를 많이 아는 아이가 감정조절도 잘한다" — and knowing emotion words DEEPLY (adult-like usage) matters more than knowing many.

INTRODUCTION

Why emotion vocabulary matters:
Emotion vocabulary is not just labeling — it provides the linguistic scaffolding for emotion comprehension, communication, and regulation. Children who can name emotions are better able to:
- Recognize emotions in others (theory of mind)
- Communicate their own emotional states
- Use language to redirect emotional arousal (instead of acting out)
- Access regulatory strategies linked to specific emotional states

Emotion vocabulary dimensions studied:
1. Vocabulary SIZE: How many different emotion words a child knows
2. Vocabulary DEPTH: How precisely and adult-like a child uses those emotion words

Hypothesis: Both size and depth contribute to emotion knowledge and regulation, but their interaction may matter most.

Connection to 감정코칭 (Gottman) Step 4 — Labeling emotions:
The Gottman model requires adults to help children name emotions. But for this to work, children need pre-existing emotion vocabulary. EV-214 identifies exactly which vocabulary properties support the downstream skill of self-regulation.

METHODS

Design: Cross-sectional
Sample: N=197 German preschoolers, ages 4-6, mean 5.6 years, typical development
Setting: German kindergartens

Measures:
1. General Vocabulary: Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test (PPVT) — standardized
2. Emotion-Specific Vocabulary SIZE: Vignettes showing emotional situations → child names the emotion
   - Number of different emotion words produced = size score
3. Emotion-Specific Vocabulary DEPTH: How adult-like and precise the emotion word use is
   - Rated by comparison to adult norms = depth score
4. Emotion Knowledge: Composite of emotion recognition + situational emotion understanding tasks
5. Behavioral Emotion Regulation: Balloons Game (computer-based)
   - Positive ER: can delay gratification, maintain positive affect appropriately
   - Negative ER: can manage frustration and negative affect

Statistical analysis: Stepwise regression with interaction terms (size × depth); robust regression

RESULTS

Emotion Knowledge:
- Emotion-specific vocabulary SIZE: significant positive predictor (β = .32, p < .001)
- Emotion-specific vocabulary DEPTH: significant positive predictor (β = .28, p < .001)
- General vocabulary: significant but smaller contribution
- Interaction SIZE × DEPTH: NOT significant for emotion knowledge
- Key finding: "emotion-specific vocabulary explains unique variance in emotion knowledge beyond general vocabulary"
- Depth compensation: "vocabulary depth compensated for smaller vocabulary size" — children with few but precisely used emotion words showed comparable emotion knowledge to children with larger but imprecisely used vocabularies

Positive Emotion Regulation:
- Main effects: vocabulary size and depth alone = NOT significant
- Interaction SIZE × DEPTH: SIGNIFICANT (β = .21, p < .01)
- Interpretation: "knowing many emotion words might be detrimental to effective ER unless combined with a more adult-like understanding"
- Having many emotion words + shallow understanding = poor regulation
- Having few but deeply understood emotion words = adequate regulation
- Optimal: many words + deep understanding = best regulation
- No main effects on negative emotion regulation

Mediation test:
- Emotion knowledge did NOT mediate the vocabulary-emotion regulation relationship
- Contrary to theoretical prediction: vocabulary seems to act directly on regulation, not only through knowledge pathway
- Alternative mechanism: language use for self-regulation (self-talk, labeling) may be more direct

DISCUSSION

The depth > size insight:
Simply knowing many emotion word labels is not sufficient and may even be counterproductive. What matters is USING them accurately — matching the word to the appropriate emotional state with adult-like precision.

Practical implication: "감정 단어를 많이 가르치는 것보다 정확하게 사용하도록 돕는 것이 더 중요하다"

Why depth compensates for size:
- A child who knows only "기쁨/슬픔/화" but uses them precisely can navigate most emotional situations
- A child who knows "기쁨/슬픔/화/두려움/질투/부끄러움/자랑스러움/당혹감" but uses them loosely is linguistically rich but emotionally confused
- The Goldilocks principle: depth of a smaller repertoire > breadth without depth

Parent/Teacher implications:
- Do not rush to introduce complex emotion vocabulary
- Ensure mastery of basic emotion words before expanding
- Model adult-like usage: "나는 지금 실망했어 — 기대했는데 안 됐거든" vs. simply "나는 슬퍼"
- Correct gentle misuse: when child says "화났어" when actually scared → "그건 화가 난 것처럼 보이기도 하는데, 혹시 무섭기도 한 거 아닐까?"

Connection to 감정코칭:
감정코칭 Step 4 (이름 붙이기):
- EV-214 shows that the labeling PRECISION matters
- The goal is not just putting a word on the feeling but using the right-enough word with accuracy
- Parents who model emotion language precisely are building the depth component

Limitations:
- Cross-sectional: cannot establish causality
- German sample: cultural generalizability uncertain (but Korean emotion vocabulary research aligns directionally)
- Behavioral ER measured only by one computer task
- Age range narrow (4-6 years)
- Emotion vocabulary measure is production-based (may underestimate comprehension)

CONCLUSION

Emotion-specific vocabulary — particularly depth of understanding — uniquely predicts emotion knowledge and positive emotion regulation in preschoolers beyond general vocabulary. The interaction between size and depth is critical: many shallow emotion words may be less beneficial than fewer deeply understood ones. Early childhood programs should prioritize precise, scaffolded emotion vocabulary instruction.
