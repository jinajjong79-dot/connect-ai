# Digital Media Use and Screen Time Exposure Among Youths: A Lifestyle-Based Public Health Concern

**Authors:** Muhammad I. Khanani; Muhammad R. Khan; Mohammed F. Farooqi; Jawad Fazal; Zainul Aabideen; Najla S. Alkuwaiti

**Affiliations:** Burjeel Royal Hospital, Al Ain, UAE; Sheikh Tahnoon bin Mohammed Medical City and Tawam Hospital, Al Ain, UAE; Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences, UAE University; Burjeel Medical City, Abu Dhabi, UAE; Pediatrics, Tawam Hospital, Al Ain, UAE

**Journal:** Cureus. 2025 Jul 20;17(7):e88373

**DOI:** 10.7759/cureus.88373

**PMC:** PMC12364383 | **PMID:** 40837898

---

The pervasive rise of digital media and screen-based entertainment has transformed the developmental landscape for children and adolescents. The COVID-19 pandemic further amplified screen exposure, exacerbating public health concerns. This review examines the physical, mental, cognitive, developmental, and emotional health consequences of digital media use and excessive screen time among youth.

Physical effects include increased risks of obesity, sleep disturbances, visual impairments, and musculoskeletal pain. Mental health outcomes are equally concerning: excessive screen exposure is associated with depression, anxiety, low self-esteem, and — alarmingly — self-harm and suicidal tendencies. Screen time exceeding 2–4 hours per day is consistently linked with increased cognitive and developmental health risks.

Despite some potential benefits of high-quality, interactive content, the evidence highlights the need for balanced media use, age-appropriate limits, and active parental guidance.

**Keywords:** adolescents, children, digital media, health outcomes, screen time

---

Digital media encompasses any content or communication transmitted through internet-enabled devices. Screen time refers to the duration of time spent on passive screen-based entertainment, excluding interactive or physically active screen-based activities.

Children and adolescents today are "digital natives," having grown up in an environment saturated with electronic and digital media.

**Key statistics:**
- UK: Nearly 1 in 4 (21%) toddlers aged 3–4 owned their own tablet; 1% had their own smartphone
- US: One-half of teenagers aged 12–17 had more than four hours of daily screen time (July 2021–December 2023)
- COVID-19 pandemic: Studies reported a **52% increase** in screen exposure among youth during lockdowns
- Screen time ≥2 hours/day rose from 41.3% (pre-COVID) to 59.4% (post-COVID) in school-aged children 6–14
- Prevalence of excessive screen time among children: 10%–93.7% in high-income countries; 21%–98% in middle-income countries

Despite these official guidelines discouraging early exposure, many children encounter screen time during infancy and develop consistent screen use habits by preschool age.

---

- Nagata et al. (2023): Medium (>4–8 hrs) and high (>8 hrs) screen time → significantly increased risk of overweight/obesity vs. low screen time (U.S. adolescents 10–14, ABCD Study, N=5,797)
- Guzmán et al. (2022): Each additional hour of screen time linked to higher incidence of overweight/obesity (OR=1.16 per hour; European IDEFICS/I.Family cohort, N=4,285)
- Haghjoo et al. (2022): Meta-analysis of 44 studies — screen time increases obesity risk **1.27 times**
- Commercial TV ads promote unhealthy eating habits, increasing obesity risk

- Zhong et al. (2025): Daily screen use before bedtime linked to later bedtimes, **~50 minutes less sleep/week**, 33% higher prevalence of poor sleep quality (N=122,000+ adults)
- Nagata et al. (2023): Bedtime screen use associated with trouble falling/staying asleep (ABCD Study, N=10,280 adolescents 10–14)
- Janssen et al. (2020): Meta-analysis — screen time associated with poor sleep in infants (0–1y) and toddlers (1–2y) but not preschoolers (3–4y)
- Underlying mechanisms: melatonin suppression, mental arousal, reduced physical activity

- Alah et al. (2025): School closures increased screen time by 11.5 hours; visual acuity declined 1.7 times (Qatar students)
- Kim et al. (2025): Marked acceleration in myopia progression during COVID-19 pandemic (N=2,064 children aged 2–17)
- Zong et al. (2024): Meta-analysis of 19 studies — higher screen time linked to increased myopia risk (**OR: 2.24**)
- Adherence to 24-hour movement guidelines (limiting screen time) associated with reduced myopia risk (aOR=0.73)

- Electronic device use linked to musculoskeletal pain in neck, wrist, upper back, hips, thighs
- Tsang et al. (2023): Excessive device use and gaming addiction linked to higher pain rates (Hong Kong, N=1,000+)
- Adolescents reported more severe effects than younger children

---

- Frielingsdorf et al. (2025): Swedish study (N=3,566 adolescents) — >4–6 hours/day digital media → poor self-esteem, depression, anxiety, pain, sleep problems (dose-response relationship)
- Gao & Gao (2024): Meta-analysis (9 prospective cohort studies) — screen time significantly associated with depression risk (**OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.12–1.28**; +1 hour/day intensifies risk)
- **Gender differences:**
  - Chen et al. (2022): ≥6 hrs/day video games → significantly increased anxiety in **boys** (OR=5.12), not girls (China, N=1,331)
  - Kjellenberg et al. (2022) and Kidokoro et al. (2022): ≥5 hrs/day screen time → significantly increased anxiety/depressive symptoms in **girls** but not boys (Sweden, Japan)
- Marciano et al. (2022): Social media ↑ risk of anxiety/depression; TV associated with REDUCED anxiety — activity-specific effects

- Rasmussen et al. (2025): ≥6 hours/day screen time → suicidal ideation in females (**aRRR: 1.67**, 95% CI: 1.44–1.93); suicide attempt in both sexes (Danish national cohort, N=28,613)
- Chu et al. (2023): Each additional hour of daily screen time → **9% increase** in odds of suicidal behaviors two years later (ABCD Study, N=11,633 children 9–11)
- McAllister et al. (2021): Girls spending >3 hours daily on social media had significantly higher rates of self-harm and depression (UK)
- Twenge & Campbell (2018): >3 hours/day electronic devices → **34% higher risk** of at least one suicide-related outcome (N=200,000+ U.S. adolescents; dose-response relationship)

- Orben et al. (2022): Increased digital technology use significantly associated with decreased life satisfaction and self-image in adolescent girls (UK Millennium Cohort Study, N=17,409)
- Sampasa-Kanyinga et al. (2022): ≥2 hours/day recreational screen time → poor mental health, low life satisfaction (N=28,000+ Canadian adolescents)
- Boer et al. (2020): Girls more negatively affected by social media in relation to life/body dissatisfaction and social anxiety (29 countries)
