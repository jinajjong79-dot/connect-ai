=== OVERVIEW ===

John Bowlby (1907-1990) is the founder of Attachment Theory, one of the most empirically supported and clinically influential theories in developmental psychology. His three-volume work "Attachment and Loss" (1969, 1973, 1980) established that human infants have a biologically based need for close emotional proximity to a primary caregiver, and that the quality of this attachment relationship shapes emotional, social, and cognitive development throughout life.

This document synthesizes Bowlby's core theory with modern attachment research, with specific application to early childhood (0-6 years) parenting.

=== THE EVOLUTIONARY BASIS OF ATTACHMENT ===

Bowlby's central insight was evolutionary: Attachment is not learned from reinforcement (Freud's cupboard love theory) but is a BIOLOGICALLY PROGRAMMED behavioral system.

The attachment system evolved because:
- Human infants are born helpless, requiring years of caregiving
- Infants who maintained proximity to caregivers survived predation and environmental dangers
- The attachment system kept infants close to caregivers during times of threat
- Over evolutionary time, this system became hardwired in human neurobiology

The attachment system is activated by:
- Physical threat (danger, pain)
- Separation from caregiver
- Illness or fatigue
- Frightening events
- Unfamiliarity

When activated, the attachment system drives proximity-seeking behavior — crying, reaching, following, clinging.

=== THE TWO FACES OF ATTACHMENT: SECURE BASE + SAFE HAVEN ===

Bowlby identified two complementary functions of the attachment figure:

1. THE SAFE HAVEN (Emotional Refuge)
- When the child is distressed, the attachment figure provides comfort, protection, and reassurance
- Safe haven function activates during threat
- Behavioral manifestation: Child runs to caregiver when hurt, scared, or overwhelmed
- Successful safe haven experience: Child feels distress regulated and returns to calm
- Failed safe haven experience: Caregiver unavailable, rejecting, or frightening → child learns proximity does not resolve distress

2. THE SECURE BASE (Platform for Exploration)
- When the child feels safe, the attachment figure provides a foundation from which to explore
- Secure base function activates during calm/safety
- Behavioral manifestation: Child confidently explores environment, periodically checking back to caregiver
- Secure base allows: Learning, play, social engagement, curiosity
- Failed secure base: Child clings, cannot explore independently, OR explores without checking back (detachment)

Bowlby's key insight: EXPLORATION and ATTACHMENT are complementary systems
- When attachment system is activated (threat/distress) → exploration STOPS → proximity-seeking begins
- When attachment system is deactivated (feeling safe) → exploration BEGINS

APPLICATION FOR PARENTS:
This explains why children "fall apart" at home after a difficult day at preschool — they have maintained regulation all day (suppressing attachment needs) and release them in the safe haven of home. "집에서 폭발하는 이유": The home is the child's safe haven. Behavioral problems at home often reflect TRUST in the parent (safe enough to fall apart), not defiance.

=== INTERNAL WORKING MODELS (IWMs) ===

Bowlby proposed that children develop "Internal Working Models" (IWMs) — cognitive-emotional representations of:
1. The self ("Am I worthy of care and love?")
2. The attachment figure ("Is my caregiver available and responsive?")
3. The relationship ("When I'm distressed, will seeking comfort work?")

These mental models develop through repeated experiences of:
- Seeking proximity
- Receiving (or not receiving) responsive caregiving
- The pattern becoming internalized as an expectation

IWMs become:
- Increasingly stable through development
- Templates for all subsequent relationships (friendships, romantic relationships, parenting)
- Self-fulfilling in social interactions (anxious attachment → behaviors that elicit rejection)

Key property of IWMs: They operate AUTOMATICALLY (outside conscious awareness) and are resistant to change — but are modifiable through significant relationship experiences.

=== MARY AINSWORTH: THE STRANGE SITUATION ===

Mary Ainsworth's empirical research built on Bowlby's theory to classify attachment patterns:

The Strange Situation Procedure (SSP):
- Laboratory paradigm involving brief separations and reunions with caregiver
- Assesses infant behavior during separation (protest vs. no protest) and reunion (seeking comfort vs. avoidance)

Four Attachment Patterns identified:

1. SECURE ATTACHMENT (Type B) — ~60% in Western samples
Characteristics:
- Uses caregiver as secure base for exploration
- Protests separation (shows distress)
- SEEKS CAREGIVER on reunion and is comforted
- Quickly returns to play after reunion
Caregiver behavior: Consistently sensitive and responsive; available when child signals need
Long-term outcomes: Emotional regulation, social competence, academic achievement, mental health

2. ANXIOUS/AMBIVALENT ATTACHMENT (Type C) — ~15-20%
Characteristics:
- Clingy; limited exploration even before separation
- Intense protest during separation
- On reunion: seeks comfort BUT RESISTANT (angry, not easily soothed)
- Hyperactivates attachment system (demanding, clingy)
Caregiver behavior: Inconsistently responsive; sometimes available, sometimes not; unpredictable
Long-term outcomes: Anxiety, difficulty with emotion regulation, demanding behavior

3. AVOIDANT ATTACHMENT (Type A) — ~20-25%
Characteristics:
- Apparently independent; explores without checking back
- Little protest during separation
- On reunion: AVOIDS caregiver (turns away, ignores)
- Deactivates attachment system (appears self-sufficient)
Caregiver behavior: Consistently REJECTING of attachment needs; discourages distress expression
Long-term outcomes: Emotional detachment, externalizing problems, difficulty with intimacy

4. DISORGANIZED ATTACHMENT (Type D) — ~15-25% in high-risk samples
Characteristics:
- No coherent strategy for managing distress
- Contradictory behaviors (approach-avoidance)
- Freezing, dazed expression, bizarre behaviors at reunion
- Caregiver is BOTH safe haven AND source of fear (the attachment dilemma)
Caregiver behavior: Frightening, frightened, or dissociated; often related to unresolved trauma/loss
Long-term outcomes: Highest risk for psychopathology; dissociation; relationship problems

=== THE TRANSMISSION OF ATTACHMENT PATTERNS ===

Bowlby and subsequent researchers established that attachment patterns are transmitted intergenerationally:

Parent's IWM → Parenting behavior → Child's attachment pattern

The Adult Attachment Interview (AAI) predicts infant attachment:
- Secure/Autonomous adult AAI → Secure infant attachment (Type B)
- Dismissing adult AAI → Avoidant infant attachment (Type A)
- Preoccupied adult AAI → Ambivalent infant attachment (Type C)
- Unresolved/Disorganized adult AAI → Disorganized infant attachment (Type D)

The "transmission gap": Parental AAI predicts ~75% of infant attachment — with sensitivity as only partial mediator. Other mechanisms: parental emotion regulation, reflective functioning, intrusive/withdrawal behaviors.

Parental reflective functioning (mentalizing):
- The ability to understand child's behavior in terms of mental states (emotions, desires, beliefs)
- High reflective functioning → more accurate empathy → responsive caregiving → secure attachment
- Reflective functioning can be enhanced through intervention (Attachment and Biobehavioral Catch-up, Circle of Security programs)

=== ATTACHMENT AND THE BRAIN ===

Modern neuroscience has elucidated biological mechanisms of attachment:

Early attachment experiences shape:
1. Stress response system (HPA axis)
   - Secure attachment → modulated cortisol response
   - Insecure/disorganized attachment → dysregulated cortisol (hyper- or hypo-reactive)

2. Autonomic nervous system
   - Secure attachment → higher vagal tone → better emotion regulation
   - (Connects to Gottman's meta-emotion research findings)

3. Prefrontal cortex development
   - Responsive caregiving supports prefrontal cortex maturation
   - Prefrontal cortex = seat of emotion regulation, decision-making, empathy

4. Stress response calibration
   - Children with unresponsive caregiving develop altered stress thresholds
   - Some become hyperreactive; others develop blunted stress responses
   - These altered baselines predict mental and physical health outcomes in adulthood

=== ATTACHMENT AND EMOTIONAL REGULATION ===

The attachment relationship IS the child's first emotion regulation system:

Infant → Experiences distress → Signals to caregiver → Caregiver regulates → Infant learns to self-regulate

This process, called "co-regulation," is the developmental precursor to self-regulation:
- Through hundreds of repetitions, child internalizes the regulatory functions of the caregiver
- This internalization forms the basis of independent emotion regulation
- Disrupted or inadequate co-regulation → impaired self-regulation capacity

Critical implication:
Children who lack secure attachment do not simply have attachment problems — they have EMOTION REGULATION deficits rooted in disrupted co-regulation. This manifests as:
- Persistent tantrums in preschool/early school age
- Difficulty calming after upset
- Extreme separation distress
- Aggressive or oppositional behavior
- Social difficulties with peers

=== ATTACHMENT SECURITY AND SCHOOL READINESS ===

Research consistently shows secure attachment predicts school readiness:
- Better emotion regulation in classroom settings
- More positive teacher relationships
- Greater curiosity and learning engagement
- Better peer relationships
- Lower rates of behavioral problems
- Greater academic achievement

The mechanism: Secure attachment → Internal working model of self as competent → More confident exploration → More learning experiences → Better academic outcomes

=== ATTACHMENT IN CLINICAL CONTEXT ===

Bowlby's original clinical insight: Emotional and behavioral problems in children often trace to disrupted attachment experiences. Rather than treating symptoms directly, addressing the attachment relationship often resolves a wide range of presenting problems.

Signs of attachment difficulties in preschool/early childhood:
- Indiscriminate affection toward strangers
- Extreme separation anxiety beyond age-expected levels
- Frozen watchfulness or hypervigilance
- Lack of affective sharing with parent
- Controlling behavior toward parent
- Excessive compliance (rigid "good" behavior)

Evidenced-based attachment interventions:
- Circle of Security (COS)
- Attachment and Biobehavioral Catch-up (ABC)
- Watch Wait and Wonder
- Child-Parent Psychotherapy (CPP) for trauma histories

=== THE SECURE BASE AT HOME: PRACTICAL IMPLICATIONS ===

For parents of young children (3-6 years), attachment theory predicts:

1. "At-home explosions" are signs of trust
   - Children regulate behavior to be acceptable at preschool (effortful control)
   - At home (safe haven), they release the accumulated stress
   - A child who "behaves everywhere except home" has a secure attachment at home
   - Response: Acknowledge the difficult day; provide comfort BEFORE addressing behavior

2. Separation anxiety is normal AND a sign of secure attachment
   - Protest at kindergarten drop-off = healthy attachment behavior
   - Complete lack of protest can indicate avoidant attachment (equally concerning)
   - Key: Predictable departure ritual + consistent return time = reduced anxiety over time

3. Exploration requires a felt sense of security
   - Children who will not separate, explore, or try new things are telling us:
     "I don't feel safe enough to explore"
   - Response: Increase felt security (more connection, predictability, warmth) — counterintuitively, NOT more encouragement to be independent

4. Physical proximity remains important through age 6
   - Elementary-aged children still need physical comfort from parents
   - Rough-and-tumble play with fathers provides physical connection + excitement regulation practice
   - Bedtime rituals serve attachment function

=== KEY QUOTES FROM BOWLBY ===

"The young child's hunger for his mother's love and presence is as great as his hunger for food, and her absence inevitably generates a powerful sense of loss and anger."

"The propensity to make intimate emotional bonds to particular individuals [is] a basic component of human nature."

"All of us, from cradle to grave, are happiest when life is organized as a series of excursions, long or short, from the secure base provided by our attachment figures."
