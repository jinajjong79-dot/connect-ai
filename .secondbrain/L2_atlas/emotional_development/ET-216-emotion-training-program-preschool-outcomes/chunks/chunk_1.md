Title: Innovative Emotion Training Program for Preschool and Kindergarten Children: Effects on Emotional Abilities and Academic Outcomes
PMC ID: PMC10670040
Year: 2023
Tier: 2 | Grade: A
Category: emotional_development
Keywords: emotion training, preschool, kindergarten, emotion recognition, emotion understanding, emotional expression, classroom intervention, low-cost, ages 3-6

---

ABSTRACT

This study examined whether structured emotion training could improve emotional abilities in 316 preschool/kindergarten children (ages 3-6) in France, and whether improvements would transfer to language and math skills. The 8-session intervention was co-developed with teachers for classroom practicality.

Key Findings:
- Significant improvements in emotion understanding and naming for ages 3-5
- No effect for ages 5-6 (ceiling effect — they already knew the material)
- Correlations between emotional abilities and academic skills (r=0.60-0.66) but no transfer
- "저비용 교실 개입으로 감정인식↑ 사회적 기술↑"

INTRODUCTION

Rationale for classroom emotion training:
- Emotion understanding and regulation are foundational for school readiness
- Preschool teachers lack structured tools for systematic emotion education
- Most emotion programs are intensive/expensive; need for accessible classroom-based approaches
- French school system provides universal access making classroom programs high-impact

The three emotion competence levels targeted:
1. Primary emotion identification: recognizing joy, sadness, fear, anger from facial/vocal/bodily cues
2. Emotion understanding: understanding emotional causes and consequences
3. Emotion expression: expressing emotions through multiple channels (face, voice, body, words)

Age-differentiated expectation:
- Ages 3-4: basic emotion identification just developing
- Ages 4-5: emotion understanding emerging
- Ages 5-6: many already have basic competencies → ceiling effects likely

METHODS

Design: Quasi-experimental (intervention vs. comparison classrooms)
Sample: N=316 children, ages 3-6
Setting: French preschool and kindergarten classrooms
Groups: 3 developmental levels
- Level 1 (CP1): Ages 3-4
- Level 2 (CP2): Ages 4-5
- Level 3 (CP3): Ages 5-6

Intervention: 8-session emotion training program
Session duration: Not specified (designed for classroom schedule)
Sequence:
- Sessions 1-3: Identifying primary emotions (joy, sadness, fear, anger)
  - Activities: emotion faces matching, emotion story sorting, emotion body cues
  - Materials: emotion cards, picture books, puppets
- Sessions 4-5: Understanding emotional causes
  - Activities: "Why does she feel sad?" causal reasoning tasks
  - Materials: situation cards, discussion prompts
- Sessions 6-8: Expressing emotions (facial, vocal, bodily channels)
  - Activities: emotion charades, emotion role-play, emotion drawing
  - Materials: mirrors, emotion journals, movement activities

Co-development process: Teachers piloted and refined activities for feasibility
Key design principle: "low-cost, classroom-practical intervention"

Measures:
- Emotion Recognition: facial expression identification tasks
- Emotion Understanding: situational understanding tasks (why does X feel Y?)
- Emotion Naming: verbal labeling of shown emotional states
- Language comprehension: standardized test
- Mathematics: standardized test

RESULTS

Emotion Training Effects by Age Level:

Level 1 (Ages 3-4): SIGNIFICANT IMPROVEMENT
- Emotion understanding: significant pre-post gain (p < .05)
- Emotion naming: significant pre-post gain (p < .05)
- "Children in the intervention group were significantly more efficient than the non-intervention group in the posttest"
- This is the highest-gain group — basic emotion skills are most plastic at 3-4

Level 2 (Ages 4-5): SIGNIFICANT IMPROVEMENT (naming)
- Emotion naming: significant improvement (p < .05)
- Emotion understanding: trend toward improvement, not significant
- Gains smaller than Level 1 (partial knowledge already present)

Level 3 (Ages 5-6): NO SIGNIFICANT EFFECT
- No significant improvements on any measure
- Interpretation: ceiling effects — 5-6 year olds already demonstrated basic competencies
- Implication: emotion training is most valuable BEFORE age 5

Correlations with Academic Skills:
- Emotional abilities × Mathematics: r = 0.66 (strong positive)
- Emotional abilities × Language comprehension: r = 0.60 (strong positive)
- These correlations replicate ET-217 (Head Start REDI) findings

Transfer to Academic Skills: NO TRANSFER
- Improved emotional abilities did NOT transfer to language or math improvements
- Contrary to hypothesis
- Important caveat: the training was 8 sessions, not an integrated year-long program
- ET-217's year-long REDI program DID show academic transfer — duration may matter

DISCUSSION

Why ages 3-4 benefit most:
- Basic emotion vocabulary and recognition are under development at 3-4
- Windows of maximal plasticity: earlier intervention = greater gains
- By 5-6, basic competencies are largely in place; training adds little

The no-transfer finding:
- 8 sessions may be insufficient to produce academic effects
- The correlation between emotion skills and academics is real (r=0.60-0.66) but intervention-based causal transfer requires sustained integration
- Recommendation: embed emotion training in academic curriculum (as REDI did) rather than as standalone sessions

Classroom teacher implementation:
- Teachers successfully delivered the program after brief training
- Low resource requirements: emotion cards, picture books, routine discussion prompts
- Can be embedded in existing morning circle time
- Does not require specialist trainers (unlike therapy-based approaches)

Korean kindergarten/preschool applicability:
- 어린이집/유치원 morning circle (아침 모임) could incorporate 2 sessions/week
- Emotion cards and picture books: inexpensive materials
- Teacher training: 1-day workshop sufficient
- Focus on ages 3-5 (maximum benefit window)

CONCLUSION

An 8-session structured emotion training program significantly improves emotion understanding and naming in children ages 3-5 in classroom settings. The program is low-cost, teacher-deliverable, and practically implementable. No transfer to academic outcomes was found, suggesting emotion training should be integrated with academic instruction for maximum benefit. Start early (before age 5) for maximum gain.
